## A patent nobody built (1714)

The story properly starts in London in 1714, when Queen Anne granted a patent to one **Henry Mill** for a "Machine for Transcribing Letters." The patent was poetic — *"an Artificial Machine or Method for the Impressing or Transcribing of Letters Singly or Progressively one after another, as in Writing, whereby all Writings whatsoever may be Engrossed in Paper or Parchment so Neat and Exact as not to be distinguished from Print"* — and we have no evidence that Mill ever actually built one. For the next century, similar patents would surface roughly once a generation, all of them prototypes, curios, or one-off experiments for the blind.

## Pellegrino Turri and Countess Carolina (c. 1808)

The earliest surviving typewritten correspondence in the world was tapped out around 1808 by **Countess Carolina Fantoni da Fivizzano**, an Italian noblewoman who was losing her sight. Her friend Pellegrino Turri built her a machine — now lost — along with sheets of carbon paper to ink the impression. (Carbon paper, that ubiquitous office staple, was invented for this single private use; it would take another sixty years to find its way into commerce.) The letters survive in an archive in Reggio Emilia. They are, as far as anyone knows, the first letters in history ever written with a typewriter.

## The Writing Ball and the Sholes & Glidden (1865–1874)

For most of the 19th century, "typewriter" meant a one-off experiment. Two events changed that.

In 1865 the Danish pastor **Rasmus Malling-Hansen** unveiled the *Skrivekugle* — the **Writing Ball** — a beautiful brass-and-rosewood instrument with 52 keys arranged radially over a hemispherical bed. It actually worked, it actually sold, and **Friedrich Nietzsche** wrote some of his late works on one when his eyesight began to fail. Of his Writing Ball he remarked: *"Our writing tools are also working on our thoughts."* Anyone who has noticed how their own writing changes between pen, screen, and typewriter will recognise the truth in that.

Then in 1868 an American newspaperman, **Christopher Latham Sholes**, patented a flat-bed machine with the typebars striking from below. He spent the next five years tinkering with the key layout — partly to slow typists down so the bars wouldn't jam, partly to keep frequently-paired letters far apart in the basket — and arrived at the layout we now know as **QWERTY**. (The "typists were deliberately slowed down" story is half-true; the real reason was mechanical, not human.) In 1873 he licensed the design to **E. Remington & Sons**, who were primarily a maker of rifles and sewing machines and had the metalwork capability to manufacture it. The **Sholes & Glidden** typewriter went on sale in 1874. It typed only in capitals; it was painted with floral decals borrowed from Remington's sewing-machine designs; and it changed clerical work forever.

## Mark Twain delivers the first typewritten manuscript (1883)

The first author to submit a typewritten manuscript to a publisher was **Mark Twain**. He claimed it was *Tom Sawyer*, but the dates don't quite work — the manuscript he typed himself appears to have been *Life on the Mississippi* in 1883. Twain was not a fan. He typed a famous letter to Remington saying he had bought the machine, hated it, and would never recommend it, *"because I don't want people to think I am operating an advertising bureau for it."* Remington promptly put the letter in an advertisement.

## The first hit machine: Remington No. 2 (1878)

The Sholes & Glidden was a curiosity. The **Remington No. 2** of 1878 was the breakthrough — the first machine to print both upper and lower case, via a clever shift mechanism that lifted the whole carriage. Within a decade the typewriter had reshaped commercial life. Until then, business correspondence was copied by hand by male clerks. The machine arrived just as women's-rights advocates were arguing for paid work outside the home, and offices abruptly hired women in their tens of thousands — they were called **"typewriters,"** the word meaning both the machine and its operator, before it settled on just the machine. By 1910 over 80% of stenographers and typists in the United States were women. It is one of those rare moments when a piece of technology and a social movement reach for each other across history and clasp hands.

## The visible typewriter (1893–1900)

There was a subtle problem with the early machines: the typist couldn't see what they had just typed. The typebars struck on the underside of the platen, and you had to lift the carriage to inspect your work. **Frank Wagner**'s patents in the late 1880s solved this by making the typebars strike from the front. The **Underwood No. 1** (1895), built around Wagner's designs, was the first truly *visible* typewriter, and over the next twenty years every serious manufacturer copied the layout. The "Underwood pattern" — front-strike, four-bank QWERTY, carriage return on the left — is what almost every machine in our [A–Z archive](/archive) descends from.

## Portables: the office in a suitcase (1908–1912)

Until this point typewriters were heavy office furniture. Two American makers changed that. **Standard Typewriter Company** released the **Corona** in 1908, then refined it into the wonderful **Corona 3 Folding** of 1912 — the carriage folded forward onto the keyboard, the whole machine fit into a small leather case, and journalists carried them to the trenches of the First World War. Hemingway typed *A Farewell to Arms* on a Corona. Edith Wharton wrote on one. The Corona 3 is the matriarch of every machine on our **Portable** shelf today.

## The golden age of European craft (1930s–1960s)

The era most of our archive comes from. As American makers consolidated into a handful of giants (Underwood, Royal, Smith-Corona, Remington), European manufacturers competed on engineering finesse and industrial design.

**Olivetti** of Ivrea, Italy, hired Marcello Nizzoli as their chief designer in 1938 and produced what may be the most beautiful office machines ever made: the **Lexikon 80** desktop and the **Lettera 22** portable (1950), which won the *Compasso d'Oro* in 1954 and was named "best design product of the last hundred years" by the Illinois Institute of Technology in 1959. Later, **Ettore Sottsass** designed the impossible-to-ignore **Valentine** (1969) in fire-engine red ABS plastic; it now lives in the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

**Hermes** of Switzerland built the **Hermes 2000** desktop — Tom Hanks's favourite — and the famously tough **Hermes Baby** ultraportable, which shipped to every corner of the British Empire and was reputedly the typewriter most often dropped from a great height and still able to type.

**Olympia** of Erfurt (later West Berlin) produced the **SM** and **SF** series — workhorse portables that turn up on our bench by the dozen.

**Consul**, in Brno (then Czechoslovakia), made the unfussy **232** — affordable, mid-century-modern in styling, and beloved enough that several have passed through Retrotype's workshop over the years in [duck-egg blue, turquoise and grey](/archive#letter-C) before being rehomed.

**Antares**, **Erika** (Saxony), **Adler** (Frankfurt), **Princess** (Bavaria) — all built machines on the same broad design template but with their own quirks of finish, action and typeface.

Britain's contribution was the **Empire Aristocrat** of West Bromwich (1956), often called the British Hermes Baby. We think the body and case are actually a touch sturdier than the Swiss original.

## Famous machines, famous writers

A short tour of the great writer-typewriter marriages:

* **Agatha Christie** wrote over 80 books on a Remington Home Portable that lives in the Greenway House museum in Devon.
* **George Orwell** typed *Nineteen Eighty-Four* on a Remington Home Portable No. 2 in a cold farmhouse on the Isle of Jura, dying of tuberculosis. The machine survives.
* **Ernest Hemingway** wrote standing up at a Royal Quiet De Luxe placed on top of a chest of drawers in his Cuban house. He kept it permanently set to single-spaced lines so he could fit more onto a page before changing the sheet.
* **Sylvia Plath** typed *The Bell Jar* on an Olivetti Lettera 22.
* **Cormac McCarthy** wrote five novels (including *Blood Meridian* and *No Country for Old Men*) on a single **Olivetti Lettera 32** he bought secondhand in 1963 for $50. When the keys finally wore out in 2009 he sold it at Christie's for **$254,500** — and bought another second-hand Lettera 32 to replace it.
* **Hunter S. Thompson** owned a series of IBM Selectrics. He kept one above his fireplace in Aspen with a bullet hole through the platen.
* **Tom Hanks** has a collection of over 250 typewriters, regularly sends new owners typewritten letters via his charity, and has written a book of short stories — *Uncommon Type* — in which each story features a typewriter.

## The IBM Selectric and the Cold War (1961–1980s)

In 1961 IBM launched the **Selectric** — a typewriter with no typebars and no moving carriage. Instead, a golf-ball-shaped element (the "type element") spun and tilted to strike the paper. You could swap the ball to change typeface mid-document; the carriage stayed put while the ball trundled left and right. It was an absolute revelation, especially in offices, and IBM sold them for decades.

The Selectric also has the distinction of being at the heart of one of the great Cold War espionage stories. Between 1976 and 1984, Soviet intelligence successfully bugged **at least 16 IBM Selectrics** used inside the U.S. embassy in Moscow and the consulate in Leningrad. Tiny magnetometers, hidden inside the typewriter mechanism, recorded the position of the type element with each keystroke and transmitted them via burst radio to listening posts nearby. American intelligence didn't catch it until a French ally tipped them off in 1984. By then the Soviets had been reading every typed document — including classified cables — for eight years. It remains, to this day, one of the most ingenious technical-intelligence operations in history.

There is a corollary: by the 1980s, Western embassies and intelligence services across the Eastern Bloc were quietly going back to *manual* typewriters precisely because they couldn't be bugged in the same way. As late as 2013 the Russian Federal Protective Service was reported to have placed an order for new mechanical typewriters in response to the Snowden revelations. Old technology, properly used, sometimes outflanks new.

## The end of the line (1980s–2000s)

The personal computer arrived. By 1985 you could buy a word processor that let you edit before you committed ink to paper — a luxury no mechanical typewriter could match. Manufacturers tried to fight back with **electronic typewriters** (daisy-wheel printers with a one-line LCD), and Brother kept making manual portables in factories in Asia well into the 1990s, but the writing was on the wall. **Smith Corona** filed for bankruptcy in 1995. **Olivetti** stopped making typewriters in the early 2000s. **Godrej & Boyce** of Mumbai, who had been making manual typewriters in India under licence since 1955, produced the last one in their factory in April 2011 — widely reported at the time as "the world's last typewriter."

For a while it seemed the story was over.

## The revival (2010–present)

It wasn't. Around 2010 something shifted. Used machines that had been sitting in attics started turning up at flea markets at rising prices. Writers spoke publicly about preferring them. The **Typosphere**, a sprawling community of typewriter bloggers, sprang up online. **Type-In** events — gatherings where strangers meet in cafes and pubs to type on each other's machines — became a regular feature in cities from Portland to Berlin. Authors began producing whole novels on typewriters again, partly for the romance, partly because, as one of them put it, a typewriter has no Twitter notifications.

Today there is a small but committed worldwide network of restorers, collectors and writers keeping these machines alive. Some manufacturers — including Royal's present-day Mexican line and a few small workshops in Eastern Europe — still assemble new manuals from old parts. Most of us, though, work on the old ones — cleaning sixty years of dust out of a segment, replacing a snapped drawstring, easing the escapement until the keys feel light again.

That is the workshop Retrotype lives in. Every machine in the [archive](/archive) is a small entry in this longer story — usually a beautiful object designed by someone you've never heard of, lovingly built, used by a stranger for half a century, and now, with luck, on someone else's desk for the next half.

> *"Our writing tools are also working on our thoughts."* — Friedrich Nietzsche, of his Writing Ball, 1882

## Further reading

For anyone who's caught the bug, a small bibliography of where to dive deeper:

- **Richard Polt**, *The Typewriter Revolution: A Typist's Companion for the 21st Century* (Countryman Press, 2015) — the standard modern reference, and a genuinely fun read.
- **Wilfred Beeching**, *Century of the Typewriter* (Heinemann, 1974) — a classic. Out of print, but worth tracking down secondhand.
- **Bruce Bliven Jr.**, *The Wonderful Writing Machine* (Random House, 1954) — slightly dated but charming.
- **Tom Hanks**, *Uncommon Type: Some Stories* (Knopf, 2017) — short fiction; each story features a typewriter.
- The [Typewriter Database](https://typewriterdatabase.com) — invaluable for dating any machine you stumble across.
- Richard Polt's [Classic Typewriter Page](https://site.xavier.edu/polt/typewriters/index.html) — model-by-model history with thousands of photographs.
