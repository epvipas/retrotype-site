## A patent nobody built (1714)

The story properly starts in London in 1714, when Queen Anne granted a patent to one **Henry Mill** for a "Machine for Transcribing Letters." The patent was poetic — "an Artificial Machine or Method for the Impressing or Transcribing of Letters Singly or Progressively one after another, as in Writing, whereby all Writings whatsoever may be Engrossed in Paper or Parchment so Neat and Exact as not to be distinguished from Print" — and we have no evidence that Mill ever actually built one. For the next century, similar patents would surface roughly once a generation, all of them prototypes, curios, or experiments for the blind.

## Pellegrino Turri and Countess Carolina (c. 1808)

The earliest surviving typewritten correspondence in the world was tapped out around 1808 by **Countess Carolina Fantoni da Fivizzano**, who was losing her sight. Her friend Pellegrino Turri built her a machine — now lost — along with sheets of carbon paper to ink the impression. The letters still exist in an archive in Reggio Emilia. They are, as far as anyone knows, the first letters in history ever written with a typewriter.

## The Writing Ball and the Sholes & Glidden (1865-1874)

For most of the 19th century, "typewriter" meant a one-off experiment. Two events changed that.

In 1865 the Danish pastor **Rasmus Malling-Hansen** unveiled the *Skrivekugle* — the **Writing Ball**, a beautiful brass-and-rosewood instrument with 52 keys arranged radially over a hemispherical bed. It actually worked, it actually sold, and Friedrich Nietzsche wrote some of his late works on one when his eyesight began to fail. He famously remarked of it: *"Our writing tools are also working on our thoughts."*

Then in 1868 an American newspaperman, **Christopher Latham Sholes**, patented a flat-bed machine with the typebars striking from below. He spent the next five years tinkering with the key layout — partly to slow typists down so the bars wouldn't jam, partly to keep frequently-paired letters far apart on the basket — and arrived at the layout we now know as **QWERTY**. In 1873 he licensed the design to **E. Remington & Sons**, who were primarily a maker of rifles and sewing machines and had the metalwork to manufacture it. The **Sholes & Glidden** typewriter went on sale in 1874. It typed only in capitals; it was painted with floral decals; and it changed clerical work forever.

## The first hit machine: Remington No. 2 (1878)

The Sholes & Glidden was a curiosity. The **Remington No. 2** of 1878 was the breakthrough — the first machine to print both upper and lower case, via a clever shift mechanism that lifted the whole carriage. Within a decade the typewriter had reshaped commercial life: women, hired in their tens of thousands as "typewriters" themselves (the word meant both the machine and its operator), arrived in the office, and the manuscript era of business correspondence ended forever.

## The visible typewriter (1893-1900)

There was a subtle problem with the early machines: the typist couldn't see what they had just typed. The typebars struck on the underside of the platen, and you had to lift the carriage to inspect your work. **Frank Wagner**'s patents in the late 1880s solved this by making the typebars strike from the front. The **Underwood No. 1** (1895), built around Wagner's designs, was the first truly *visible* typewriter, and over the next twenty years every serious manufacturer copied the layout. The "Underwood pattern" — front-strike, four-bank QWERTY, carriage return on the left — is what almost every machine in our archive descends from.

## Portables: the office in a suitcase (1909-1912)

Until this point typewriters were heavy office furniture. Two American makers changed that:

* **Standard Typewriter Company** released the **Corona** in 1908, then refined it into the wonderful **Corona 3 Folding** of 1912 — the carriage folded forward onto the keyboard, the whole machine fit into a small leather case, and journalists carried them to the trenches of the First World War.
* **Smith Premier** and others followed with their own portables.

It's hard to overstate how liberating this was. A correspondent in Flanders could file copy from a dugout. Hemingway typed *A Farewell to Arms* on a Corona. The Corona 3 is the matriarch of every machine on our **Portable** shelf today.

## The golden age of European craft (1930s-1960s)

The era most of our archive comes from. As American makers consolidated into a handful of giants (Underwood, Royal, Smith-Corona, Remington), European manufacturers competed on engineering finesse and industrial design. A short tour:

**Olivetti** of Ivrea, Italy, hired Marcello Nizzoli as their chief designer in 1938 and produced what may be the most beautiful office machines ever made: the **Lexikon 80** desktop and the **Lettera 22** portable (1950), which won the *Compasso d'Oro* in 1954 and was named "best design product of the last hundred years" by the Illinois Institute of Technology in 1959. Later, **Ettore Sottsass** designed the impossible-to-ignore **Valentine** (1969) in fire-engine red ABS plastic.

**Hermes** of Switzerland built the **Hermes 2000** desktop — Tom Hanks's favourite, and the seed of our [Hermes Collection](/info#hermes) — and the famously tough **Hermes Baby** ultraportable, which weighed almost nothing and shipped to every corner of the British Empire.

**Olympia** of Erfurt (later West Berlin) produced the **SM and SF** series — workhorse portables that turn up on our bench by the dozen.

**Consul**, in Brno (then Czechoslovakia), made the unfussy **232** — affordable, mid-century-modern in styling, and beloved enough that we now stock it in [duck-egg blue, turquoise and grey](/archive#letter-C).

**Antares**, **Erika** (Saxony), **Adler** (Frankfurt), **Princess** (Bavaria) — all built machines on the same broad design template but with their own quirks of finish, action and typeface.

Britain's contribution was the **Empire Aristocrat** of West Bromwich (1956), often called the British Hermes Baby. We think the body and case are actually a touch sturdier than the Swiss original.

## The IBM Selectric and the electric age (1961)

In 1961 IBM launched the **Selectric** — a typewriter with no typebars and no moving carriage. Instead, a golf-ball-shaped element ("type element") spun and tilted to strike the paper. You could swap the ball to change typeface mid-document; the carriage stayed put while the ball trundled left and right. It was an absolute revelation, especially in offices, and IBM sold them for decades. Mechanical portables lived on for the home and the road, but in the workplace the Selectric ruled the 60s and 70s.

## The end of the line (1980s-2000s)

The personal computer arrived. By 1985 you could buy a word processor that let you edit before you committed ink to paper — a luxury no mechanical typewriter could match. Manufacturers tried to fight back with **electronic typewriters** (daisy-wheel printers with a one-line LCD), and Brother kept making manual portables in factories in Asia well into the 1990s, but the writing was on the wall. **Smith Corona** filed for bankruptcy in 1995. **Olivetti** stopped making typewriters in the early 2000s. The last machine on our most recent Underwood line came off in 1995.

For a while it seemed the story was over.

## The revival (2010-present)

It wasn't. Around 2010 something shifted. Used machines that had been sitting in attics started turning up at flea markets at rising prices. Writers — Tom Hanks, Cormac McCarthy, Don DeLillo, John Updike — talked publicly about preferring them. The **Typosphere**, a sprawling community of typewriter bloggers, sprang up online. Authors began producing whole novels on them, partly for the romance, partly because a typewriter has no Twitter notifications.

Today there is a small but committed worldwide network of restorers, collectors and writers keeping these machines alive. Some, like the **Russian Bashkir** factory and **Royal**'s present-day Mexican line, still produce new manuals. Most of us work on the old ones — cleaning sixty years of dust out of a segment, replacing a snapped drawstring, easing the escapement until the keys feel light again.

That is the workshop Retrotype lives in. Every machine in the [archive](/archive) is a small entry in this longer story — usually a beautiful object designed by someone you've never heard of, lovingly built, used by a stranger for half a century, and now, with luck, on someone else's desk for the next half.

> *"Our writing tools are also working on our thoughts."* — Friedrich Nietzsche, of his Writing Ball, 1882

## Further reading

For anyone who's caught the bug, a small bibliography of where to dive deeper:

- **Richard Polt**, *The Typewriter Revolution: A Typist's Companion for the 21st Century* (Countryman Press, 2015) — the standard modern reference, and a genuinely fun read.
- **Wilfred Beeching**, *Century of the Typewriter* (Heinemann, 1974) — a classic. Out of print, but worth tracking down secondhand.
- **Bruce Bliven Jr.**, *The Wonderful Writing Machine* (Random House, 1954) — slightly dated but charming.
- The [Typewriter Database](https://typewriterdatabase.com) — invaluable for dating any machine you stumble across.
- Richard Polt's [Classic Typewriter Page](https://site.xavier.edu/polt/typewriters/index.html) — model-by-model history with thousands of photographs.
